Sleep Cycles Explained

What happens in each 90-minute sleep cycle, why deep and dream sleep matter, and how cycles change across the night.

Sleep is not one long stretch of unconsciousness. It is a repeating cycle of stages, each lasting about 90 minutes, and each doing a different job. Understanding the cycle explains why some mornings feel easy and others feel terrible, why the first hours of sleep matter most for the body, and why the last hours matter most for the mind. This guide walks through a night, cycle by cycle.

The four stages of sleep

Each cycle has four stages. The first is light sleep, the transition from waking, when you can be woken easily. The second is a slightly deeper light sleep, with slower brain waves and a lower heart rate. The third is deep sleep, the most restorative stage, when the body repairs tissue, builds bone and muscle, and strengthens the immune system. The fourth is REM sleep, named for the rapid eye movements beneath closed eyelids, when most dreaming happens and the brain processes memories and emotions. A cycle moves through these stages in order, then repeats.

How cycles change across the night

Early cycles have more deep sleep and less dreaming. That is why the first three or four hours are the most physically restorative, and why a short night still gives you a useful amount of deep sleep. Later cycles have more REM sleep and lighter deep sleep, which is why the last hours matter more for mood, memory and learning. If you cut a night short from the end, you lose mostly dream sleep. If you cut it short from the start, by going to bed late, you lose deep sleep, which is harder to replace.

Why 90 minutes is an average, not a rule

Sleep cycles last about 90 minutes on average, but they vary between people and between nights, from about 70 to 110 minutes. The calculator uses 90 minutes because it is the best single figure for planning, and it works well for most people. If you consistently wake groggy at the calculated times, your cycles may be a little shorter or longer than 90 minutes, and shifting your bedtime by 15 minutes in either direction can help. A sleep tracker that records movement or heart rate can estimate your own cycle length over time.

Waking between cycles

The moment between cycles is the lightest point of sleep, and it is the best time to wake up. That is why the calculator's bedtimes are spaced 90 minutes apart: each one lands at a boundary between cycles. Waking in the middle of deep sleep, by contrast, causes sleep inertia, a groggy, confused feeling that can last 30 minutes or more. The difference between the two is often only 20 or 30 minutes of timing, which is why a calculator that aligns with cycles can make a real difference to how mornings feel.

What disturbs the cycles

Alcohol is the biggest disturber of sleep cycles: it may help you fall asleep, but it suppresses REM sleep in the first half of the night and causes fragmented sleep later. Caffeine late in the day delays sleep onset and lightens deep sleep. An irregular schedule, with bedtimes that swing by hours, confuses the body's clock and fragments cycles. Noise, light, a hot room, and a full bladder all cause brief awakenings that break the cycle pattern. Keeping a regular schedule, a dark cool room, and no alcohol or late caffeine protects the cycles better than any gadget.

A night, hour by hour

Follow a night that starts at 11 p.m., with sleep beginning at 11:15 p.m. The first cycle runs to about 12:45 a.m., rich in deep sleep. The second runs to about 2:15 a.m., still deep. The third runs to about 3:45 a.m., with deep sleep fading and dreaming beginning. The fourth runs to about 5:15 a.m., with more dreaming. The fifth runs to about 6:45 a.m., mostly light and dream sleep. Waking at 6:45 a.m. feels natural, because it lands at the end of a cycle. Waking at 6 a.m., in the middle of the fifth cycle, feels groggy, even though it is only 45 minutes earlier. The hour-by-hour picture shows why the alarm time matters as much as the total hours, and why the calculator's times are spaced 90 minutes apart.

Tip: Early cycles are rich in deep sleep, which restores the body; later cycles are rich in dream sleep, which supports memory and mood. Cutting the night short from the start loses deep sleep.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is a sleep cycle?

About 90 minutes on average, ranging from roughly 70 to 110 minutes between people and nights.

What is deep sleep for?

Repairing tissue, building bone and muscle, and strengthening the immune system. It is concentrated in the early cycles.

What is REM sleep for?

Dreaming, processing memories and emotions, and supporting mood. It is concentrated in the later cycles.
