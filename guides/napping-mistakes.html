Napping Mistakes to Avoid

The common errors that turn a refreshing nap into grogginess, night-time insomnia, or a wasted afternoon.

A nap can be the best 20 minutes of your day, or the worst. The difference is usually a handful of avoidable mistakes: napping too long, napping too late, napping in the wrong place, or napping when you should be fixing your night-time sleep instead. This guide covers the mistakes and how to avoid them.

Napping too long

The most common mistake is napping for 45 minutes to an hour, which drops you into deep sleep and leaves you groggy for half an hour afterwards. This sleep inertia is the reason many people say naps do not work for them: they tried a long nap and woke feeling worse. Keep naps to 10 to 20 minutes, or go for a full 90-minute cycle if you have the time. Anything in between is the worst of both worlds, and it is the length to avoid.

Napping too late

Napping after about 3 p.m. reduces the sleep pressure that builds up during the day, which makes it harder to fall asleep at night. The later the nap, the stronger the effect. If you are tired in the late afternoon, a short walk, bright light, or a glass of water is a better response than a nap. The best nap window is early afternoon, roughly 1 to 3 p.m., when the body naturally dips and a short nap fits the clock.

Napping in the wrong place

A nap on the sofa with the television on, in a bright room, is not a nap; it is dozing, and it rarely refreshes. A good nap needs a quiet, dark, comfortable place, like a bedroom with the curtains drawn. If you nap at work, find a quiet room, use an eye mask and earplugs, and set an alarm. The environment matters as much as the length, because light and noise keep the brain in light sleep and prevent the nap from doing its job.

Using naps to cover a short night

Naps are a supplement, not a substitute. If you sleep five hours a night and nap for an hour every afternoon, you are still short of sleep, and the nap is masking a problem rather than solving it. The fix is an earlier bedtime, not a longer nap. A nap is for a tired day, a long shift, or a new parent, not for a permanently short night. If you need a nap every day to function, your night-time sleep is probably too short or too poor, and that is the problem to fix.

Skipping the alarm

A nap without an alarm is a gamble: you may wake after 20 minutes, or you may wake after two hours, groggy and with your night ruined. Always set an alarm, for 25 minutes, which allows about 5 minutes to fall asleep and 20 minutes of sleep. Get up when it rings, step into light, and move around for a minute. The discipline of the alarm is what makes the nap work, and it is the single easiest mistake to avoid.

A nap diary for a week

Keep a nap diary for a week: each day, note whether you napped, when, for how long, and how you felt afterwards and at bedtime. After a week, the pattern is usually clear. Naps of 20 minutes in the early afternoon leave you refreshed and do not disturb the night. Naps of 45 minutes leave you groggy. Naps after 3 p.m. make the night harder. Naps on the sofa with the television on do not refresh at all. The diary turns a vague feeling about naps into evidence, and the evidence tells you exactly what to change. Most people find that one change, usually the length or the timing, fixes the problem entirely.

Tip: Keep naps to 10 to 20 minutes, or go for a full 90 minutes. Anything in between drops you into deep sleep and leaves you groggy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I feel worse after a nap?

You probably napped too long and woke from deep sleep. Keep naps to 10 to 20 minutes, or a full 90-minute cycle.

What time should I avoid napping?

After about 3 p.m. Napping late reduces the sleep pressure you need to fall asleep at night.

Can naps replace a short night?

No. A nap is a supplement, not a substitute. If you need a nap every day, your night-time sleep is too short.
