Sleep Hygiene Checklist

The everyday habits that protect sleep: light, temperature, caffeine, screens, and a regular schedule.

Sleep hygiene is the name for the everyday habits that protect sleep, and it sounds dull, but it works. Most sleep problems are made worse by a handful of habits that are easy to change: irregular hours, bright light at night, caffeine too late, and a bedroom that is too warm or too bright. This guide is a checklist you can work through in a week, with the changes that matter most first.

Keep a regular schedule

Go to bed and wake up at the same times every day, within an hour, including weekends. Regularity is the single most powerful sleep habit, because the body clock learns from repetition and starts preparing for sleep before bedtime. A regular wake time matters even more than a regular bedtime, because it anchors the clock. If you can only change one thing, change this, and keep it for two weeks before judging the result.

Light: bright mornings, dark nights

Get bright light within an hour of waking, ideally daylight outdoors for 10 to 30 minutes. It resets the clock and improves alertness. In the evening, dim the lights and avoid bright screens in the last hour before bed, because light at night suppresses melatonin, the hormone that signals sleepiness. If you must use a screen, lower the brightness and use a warm colour setting. A dark bedroom, with blackout curtains or an eye mask, protects sleep through the night.

Caffeine, alcohol and food

Caffeine lasts longer than most people think: it has a half-life of about five to six hours, so a coffee at 4 p.m. is still half there at 10 p.m. Set a caffeine cut-off, usually around midday to early afternoon, and stick to it. Alcohol may help you fall asleep, but it fragments sleep and suppresses dreaming, so avoid it in the evening if sleep matters. Eat dinner a few hours before bed, and avoid heavy, spicy meals late at night. A small snack is fine if you are hungry.

The bedroom

Keep the bedroom cool, around 18 degrees Celsius, dark, and quiet. Use the bed for sleep, not for work, television or scrolling, so the brain learns to associate it with sleep. If you cannot fall asleep after about 20 minutes, get up, go to another room, and do something quiet and dimly lit until you feel sleepy, then return to bed. Lying awake in bed trains the brain to associate the bed with wakefulness, which makes the problem worse.

Wind down before bed

Give yourself 30 to 60 minutes to wind down: dim the lights, read, stretch, take a warm bath, or listen to calm music. A warm bath works because the body cools down afterwards, and cooling is a signal for sleep. Avoid work, arguments, and exciting television in the last hour. Write down anything on your mind, so it does not keep you awake, and keep a notepad by the bed for thoughts that arrive during the night.

A one-week sleep hygiene experiment

Try the checklist as a one-week experiment. On Monday, fix your wake time and keep it all week. On Tuesday, get bright light within an hour of waking. On Wednesday, set a caffeine cut-off at midday. On Thursday, dim the lights and stop screens an hour before bed. On Friday, make the bedroom dark and cool. On Saturday, keep the same wake time despite the weekend. On Sunday, review the week: which change helped most, and which was hardest? Keep the changes that helped and drop the ones that did not, but give each one a full week before judging it, because the body clock responds slowly. The experiment turns a list of advice into personal evidence, and the evidence is what makes the habits stick.

Tip: Regularity is the most powerful sleep habit: the same bedtime and wake time every day, within an hour, including weekends.
