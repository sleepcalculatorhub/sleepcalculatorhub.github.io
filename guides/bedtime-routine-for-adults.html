A Bedtime Routine for Adults

How to build a 30 to 60 minute wind-down routine that helps you fall asleep faster and sleep better.

A bedtime routine is not just for children. Adults fall asleep faster and sleep better when the hour before bed follows a regular, calming pattern, because the brain learns to associate the routine with sleep. The routine does not need to be elaborate: a few simple steps, done in the same order each night, are enough. This guide shows how to build one that fits your life.

Why a routine works

The brain learns by association. If you do the same calming things each night before bed, the brain starts to prepare for sleep as soon as the routine begins, releasing melatonin and lowering alertness. A routine also separates the day from the night: work, news and arguments belong to the day, and the routine marks the boundary. Without a boundary, the day's worries follow you into bed, and you lie awake thinking about them.

The 30 to 60 minute wind-down

Start the wind-down 30 to 60 minutes before bed. Dim the lights, turn off screens, and switch to a calm activity: reading a paper book, stretching, taking a warm bath, or listening to quiet music. A warm bath works because the body cools down afterwards, and cooling is a signal for sleep. Prepare for tomorrow during the wind-down, not in bed: lay out clothes, write a short to-do list, and put anything you need by the door, so the morning is easy and the night is free of planning.

What to include

Choose three or four steps and do them in the same order each night. A simple routine might be: dim the lights, take a warm shower, read for 20 minutes, and write down three things on your mind. Another might be: stretch for 10 minutes, make herbal tea, read, and set the alarm. The content matters less than the consistency. Avoid screens, work, and stimulating conversation during the wind-down, and keep the bedroom for sleep, not for the routine's activities.

Dealing with a racing mind

A racing mind is the most common reason adults cannot fall asleep, and the routine can help if it includes a way to park thoughts. Keep a notepad by the bed and write down anything that comes to mind, with a note about what you will do tomorrow. This tells the brain the thought is stored and does not need to be rehearsed. If worries persist, set aside 15 minutes earlier in the evening, a worry time, to write them down and plan, so they do not arrive at bedtime.

Making it stick

A routine takes about two weeks to become automatic, so give it time. Start with a short routine, 20 to 30 minutes, and lengthen it if you enjoy it. Keep the same routine on weekends, within reason, because the brain learns from repetition. If you miss a night, simply return to the routine the next night without guilt. The goal is a pattern that signals sleep, not a perfect performance, and a simple routine you keep is better than an elaborate one you abandon.

A routine for a busy evening

Take a person who gets home at 7 p.m. and must be asleep by 11 p.m. The routine starts at 10 p.m.: dim the lights, put the phone on charge outside the bedroom, and make a herbal tea. At 10:15, take a warm shower. At 10:30, read a paper book in bed for 20 minutes. At 10:50, write down anything on your mind for tomorrow, set the alarm, and turn the lights out. The routine takes 50 minutes and fits a busy evening, because it starts late and uses simple steps. The key is the order: the same steps, in the same order, every night, so the brain learns that the shower means sleep is coming. After two weeks, the routine is automatic, and falling asleep takes minutes rather than an hour.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a bedtime routine include?

Three or four calming steps in the same order each night: dim lights, a warm bath or shower, reading, and writing down anything on your mind.

How long should a wind-down be?

30 to 60 minutes before bed. Start with 20 to 30 minutes if that feels easier, and lengthen it if you enjoy it.

What if my mind races at bedtime?

Write thoughts down on a notepad by the bed, with a note about tomorrow. It tells the brain the thought is stored.
